# Global Democratic Governance Charter
### A political circuit for world citizens, beside states rather than above them

**Second edition** (working draft, October 2026)


---

## Where this charter fits

This charter sets out the institutional design that **Proposal A, the Youth GCC**, builds on. Proposal A describes who carries it; this charter describes the institutions themselves and the principles that keep them democratic.

- **Proposal A — Youth GCC** [→](../proposals/a-youth-gcc.md): the youth-led body that founds and runs this circuit
- **Proposal B — Global Citizen Will Platform** [→](../proposals/b-platform.md): the open participation layer
- **Proposal C — Planetary OS** [→](../proposals/c-summary.md): the first policy design taken through the circuit

All names, sizes, and numbers below are **illustrative**. They show a direction, and are meant to be tested and revised.

---

## 1. Why this charter is needed

States are essential. They protect people's freedom, security, welfare, and culture. But many of the problems that will shape humanity's future cannot be solved by any state alone: climate change, advanced AI, biotechnology, nuclear weapons, pandemics, the oceans, and global finance all cross borders.

Today's international institutions mainly coordinate the interests of states. That is valuable work. But when the interests of states and the interests of humanity as a whole do not coincide, no permanent institution develops policy options from the standpoint of the world as a whole, tests them, and lets citizens judge them.

This charter aims to fill that gap. It does not abolish states, reject international organizations, or exclude companies, universities, or civil society. It adds a separate circuit in which **world citizens raise problems, develop and test options, deliberate, and approve proposals democratically**.

---

## 2. Three principles

### Principle 1: world citizens are a political subject

On problems shared by humanity, every person has interests as a world citizen, regardless of nationality. Those interests are not the same as the interests of states. So bodies that represent states and bodies that represent world citizens are kept separate. The UN and other intergovernmental bodies coordinate among states. The circuit described here develops policy for humanity's long-term interests. The two are not meant to oppose each other, but to check and cooperate with each other.

### Principle 2: making options is separate from deciding

Democracy is not only a vote. **Deciding which options are on the table is itself a form of power.** So the whole sequence is placed inside democratic institutions:

1. find the problem
2. define it
3. design several options
4. test each one scientifically and economically
5. citizens deliberate
6. citizens choose
7. try it in practice
8. evaluate the results
9. improve the policy

### Principle 3: begin as a policy-making body, not a government

At first, the circuit has **no power to compel states**. Instead, it asks states and international bodies to **respond publicly** to policies that world citizens have formed democratically, and to explain why they adopt, modify, or reject them. The circuit does not start by telling states what to do. It starts by offering proposals and asking for reasons.

## 3. The institutions

| Institution | Role (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| **World Citizens' Assembly** | Citizens selected by lot to form a statistical microcosm of the world's population, from all generations. Selection reflects region, age, gender, income, education, and urban or rural life, not national quotas. A permanent core of roughly 500–2,000 members, partly replaced each year, with a larger online participation process for major questions. Members are paid and supported with translation, equipment, learning support, childcare and elder care, and disability support, so that income and place of residence do not decide who can take part |
| **World Policy Institute** | Independent of any state, government, party, company, or international body. No single state or company may provide more than a set share of its funding, and all funding is published. For each issue it designs **at least three workable options**, and publishes their costs, effects, distributional and regional impacts, feasibility, risks, side effects, and long-term consequences |
| **World Policy Laboratory** | An independent testing unit inside the Institute. Policies are **tested as far as possible before they are scaled up**: simulation, small pilots, comparison across regions, independent evaluation, and review by the assembly. Failures are published as well as successes, as knowledge shared by everyone |
| **Global Citizens' Policy Council** | A standing body that connects the assembly and the Institute. It brings together citizen representatives, scientists, engineers, economists, legal scholars, ethicists, civil society, affected people, and practitioners. It is needed because the assembly is large and meets periodically, while the Institute is a body of experts: the Council is where the two work together continuously, framing the questions, checking that the options are fair and understandable, and keeping continuity between assembly sessions. **Final democratic authority rests with the citizen representatives.** Experts show what is possible, what may happen, and what side effects may follow; they do not decide what is desirable |
| **Independent audit** | Reviews procedures, finances, conflicts of interest, and fairness of participation, and publishes the results |

**An example of several options.** On climate, the Institute might set out options such as a carbon-price-centered design, a global carbon dividend, public investment and allocation, and a market–regulation mix. **Planetary OS** is a detailed version of the global carbon dividend option, to be compared with the others.

The four types, as examples:

- **Carbon-price-centered:** a carbon tax or emissions trading sets a price on carbon; each country decides how to use the revenue.
- **Global carbon dividend:** the revenue is returned directly to citizens.
- **Public investment and allocation:** public spending builds low-carbon infrastructure, and scarce resources are allocated where needed.
- **Market–regulation mix:** standards and regulations, such as efficiency standards and phase-out dates, combined with market mechanisms.

## 4. The policy cycle

For major policies, the institutions work in a cycle:

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Citizens raise an issue | Issues that gain enough support are formally taken up |
| 2. The evidence is organized | The Institute reviews the science, existing policies, models, technical possibilities, and ethical questions |
| 3. Options are designed | Always several, never a single "right answer" |
| 4. Options are compared | With economic models, climate models, and AI-assisted simulation. **All models, data, and assumptions are made public**, and AI output is never the basis of a decision on its own |
| 5. The assembly deliberates | Citizens hear experts, ask questions, and discuss |
| 6. The assembly decides | It adopts recommendations that include a preferred option, alternatives, minority views, and uncertainties |
| 7. A world citizens' vote, if needed | For major questions, an online vote can supplement the assembly, but only on options that have already been deliberated |
| 8. Testing | The Laboratory tests what can be tested |
| 9. International institutionalization | Results go to governments, parliaments, regional bodies such as the EU, the UN, and other international organizations |

## 5. Relations with the UN, states, and companies

**The UN.** The UN does not decide the will of world citizens on their behalf. It carries out coordination among states and international implementation. When the assembly adopts a policy that meets agreed standards, the UN General Assembly and relevant bodies are asked to consider it formally. If the UN or member states reject it, they are asked to publish their reasons. In this way, even without any veto, world citizens can have the capacity to form policy, and the legitimacy that comes with it.

**States.** States may take part, but may not control the institutions.

**Companies.** Companies can take part as stakeholders that contribute knowledge, technology, and implementation capacity. But **they receive no seats as citizen representatives**: companies take part as companies, and citizens as citizens. Funds, data, and research they provide are published, and may not be used to steer agendas, findings, or votes.

## 6. Protecting democratic legitimacy

A body like this could itself become a new elite. To prevent that, the charter builds in seven safeguards:

1. **Term limits.** No one holds power for long.
2. **Selection by lot.** Key citizen representatives are chosen by lottery rather than election.
3. **Full transparency.** Materials, methods, funding, and votes are published.
4. **Minority views on record.** A majority vote never erases a minority view.
5. **External audit.** An independent body audits the institutions.
6. **Separation of powers.** Research, deliberation, testing, audit, and international negotiation are never concentrated in one body.
7. **Reform from below.** Citizens can propose reforms of the institutions themselves.

Above all, **no one may claim to own the definition of humanity's interest.** It is treated as a hypothesis, formed and revised through continuing democratic deliberation. Not scientists alone, not citizens alone, not governments, companies, or AI alone: each has a different role.

## 7. Starting small

There is no need to build this on a global scale all at once. The circuit begins with one concrete agenda and one full run of the policy cycle.

**First agenda: climate.** Within the package, the first run is an international citizens' assembly on climate policy. The Institute's working groups draft the Planetary OS Standard and alternative options; the assembly deliberates on them; the Laboratory runs the Citizen Dividend Simulator [→](../tools/simulator.md) and prepares small pilots; and an independent evaluation reviews the whole process.

**Next: a Global AI Citizens' Assembly.**

Several hundred to about 1,000 citizens, selected at random from every region, would deliberate for six to twelve months on one question: *in light of the risks and benefits of advanced AI, what kind of AI governance should the world build?* With interpretation, science education, dialogue with experts, AI-assisted organization of materials, and policy simulation, they would produce, for example:

- principles for AI
- a proposal for international AI regulation
- an international system for monitoring AI incidents and dangerous capabilities
- rules on computing resources and data centers
- public investment in AI safety research
- a division of roles between states, companies, and international bodies

The results would go to governments, the UN, AI companies, and research institutions.

This could also run in parallel with the climate assembly if partners and resources allow.

## 8. A possible long-term path

Only after a substantial track record, the circuit could develop step by step:

| Stage | Role |
|---|---|
| 1 | A policy advisory body |
| 2 | An international policy-making body |
| 3 | A body that takes part formally in forming international treaties |
| 4 | A world citizens' parliament |

In the long run, this could lead to **a two-chamber world politics**: a chamber of states, made up of governments, and a chamber of world citizens, made up mainly of representatives selected by lot to reflect the world's population, deliberating together on global public goods. This is a direction for the long term, not a goal of the launch phase.

---

## 9. Open questions

This charter is a working hypothesis. Among the questions that remain open:

- how a few thousand citizens chosen by lot can represent the world credibly, and how that is explained to the public
- what "standards" a policy must meet before the UN is asked to consider it
- how to fund the institutions without dependence on a few large funders
- how to connect global deliberation with regional and national deliberation

These are the questions the first pilots are designed to test.

---

## Closing

The purpose of this charter is not to abolish states, exclude the great powers, or replace the United Nations. It is to respond to a 21st-century problem: **when each state acts rationally, the result can be harmful for humanity as a whole.**

Today's world has states, companies, universities, and international organizations. What it lacks is a permanent institution that develops options from the interests of humanity as a whole, tests them, and lets citizens judge them. The first role of the circuit described here is not to govern in place of states. It is to show, through research, testing, and deliberation, that **there are options states have not yet considered**. Diplomacy between states can follow.
