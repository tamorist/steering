# Proposal C: Planetary OS
## Frequently Asked Questions: common objections and our answers

**FAQ** — First edition (working draft)

### About this document

- This FAQ collects the objections Planetary OS is most likely to meet, with short answers. Each answer points to the section of the Full Proposal ("§") and the part of the companion series *Rewriting the Planet's OS* ("Part") where the reasoning is developed.
- All names, ratios, and figures are **illustrative**, as in the Full Proposal.
- Where an objection points to a problem that is not yet solved, we say so. Those points are collected in the last section.

## A. The basic idea

### A1. Isn't this just a carbon tax under a new name?

No. A carbon tax fixes the **price** and leaves the amount of emissions uncertain. Planetary OS fixes the **amount**: a cap derived from the remaining carbon budget, applied upstream and sold at auction. The price is discovered by firms bidding for allowances. Under a fixed cumulative budget, controlling the quantity is the more reliable choice. What is new is not the cap itself but what surrounds it: a cap that only moves one way, revenue returned directly to citizens, rewards for using less, a share of historical liability, and protection against repeal.
*See: §4.2, §13 / Part 3*

### A2. The EU already has an emissions trading system. How is this different?

The EU ETS was built inside a growth economy. Every tightening of the cap was felt as a pure loss, so for years the cap stayed loose and free allowances continued. Planetary OS changes three things:

- **The cap never loosens.** When the price is high, the response is a larger dividend and more support, not a looser cap.
- **The revenue goes back to citizens**, so a stricter cap does not break household budgets.
- **Using less is rewarded**, for households, firms, regions, and nations, so reducing is no longer a loss.

Together these turn public opinion from pushing the cap down to defending it.
*See: §2.2, §6.1 / Part 3*

### A3. Carbon pricing keeps being repealed. Why would this survive?

Past attempts shared six weaknesses (§2.1). Planetary OS answers each one:

- The burden is shared with past beneficiaries through BEI.
- Day-to-day adjustment runs by pre-approved rules, which leaves less room for bargaining.
- Loosening the rules requires a **double key**: scientific justification plus approval by at least three-quarters of citizens' representatives.
- A visible dividend creates a constituency that defends the system.
- It spreads as a club rather than through unilateral adoption.
- It is designed and tested before the next crisis.

None of this guarantees survival. It shifts the political balance toward keeping the system rather than repealing it.
*See: §2.1, §4.4 / Parts 1, 7*

### A4. Why give up on 1.5°C? Isn't that defeatist?

Holding warming to 1.5°C without overshoot is now very likely out of reach. Keeping a target that cannot be met erodes trust and invites the question "then why bother?" **Staying well below 2°C** is still extremely demanding, at roughly 8% a year globally. A target that is hard but achievable makes it possible to set a real budget and a real cap. Overshoot beyond the budget is partly addressed through investment in carbon removal.
*See: §3.1 / Part 2*

### A5. Cutting 8% a year is impossible. It would wreck the economy.

The path is not imposed on everyone from day one. At the club stage, the reduction rate rises with the club's size, starting at about 3% a year and moving toward the budget-derived rate as the club grows (§5.5). And "8% a year" describes the volume of fossil fuels, not the size of human well-being. Under the cap, the sufficiency rewards and the Just Transition turn part of the reduction into a gain rather than a loss. It will still be a deep transformation. The proposal does not hide that; it designs so that the transformation does not land on the people least able to bear it.
*See: §3.1, §5.5 / Parts 3, 6*

## B. Households and fairness

### B1. Higher energy prices hurt the poor most.

Without a dividend, yes. With one, the opposite tends to hold. Energy and material use rise steeply with income, so if revenue is returned in equal per-capita amounts, the majority who use less than average receive more than they pay. As a rough calculation under the standard assumptions of this proposal, **about 70% of people come out net positive**, with a range from about 60% to the low 70s depending on the assumptions. On top of that, the standard design guarantees **at least 70%** through a majority-benefit rule (see below). Lower-income households gain the most.
*See: §4.3 / Part 5*

### B2. Then who loses? What about the other 30%?

Mainly people who use more than average: higher-income households, and some people who drive long distances or live in large, poorly insulated homes. The design softens this in three ways:

- The sufficiency-linked dividend **corrects for use that people cannot easily change**: cold climates, areas with little public transport, medical or disability needs.
- The **Just Transition** (10% of auction revenue) supports workers and regions that depend on fossil industries.
- The standard design includes a **majority-benefit rule**: households just short of break-even receive a top-up from the PTE so that at least 70% come out ahead. The top-up is fixed by each household's use when it joined, so cutting back still pays, and it is limited by what the PTE can spend. The level can be revisited democratically.

Some households will pay more than they receive. That is not a failure of the design; it is the price signal doing its job, applied first to those with the greatest capacity to change.
*See: §4.3 / Part 5*

### B3. Won't people spend the dividend on more consumption and push emissions back up?

No. Under a cap, total emissions cannot rise. If dividend spending raises energy demand, the only effect is a higher allowance price, which rewards those who use less even more.
*See: §4.3 / Part 5*

### B4. Isn't the sufficiency-linked dividend a form of surveillance?

It is designed not to be:

- **Coarse data only.** It uses existing annual data such as electricity and gas totals, vehicle type, and building energy ratings. Material consumption is estimated with category-level average coefficients, never by tracking individual purchases.
- **Opt-in.** People who prefer not to take part receive a standard value and still get the universal dividend.
- **Data minimization.** Data is held only as long as needed to calculate the payment.

If sufficiency action points are added in the future, purchase records stay on the user's own device, and only an annual total is reported.

The cap itself applies upstream, at a few thousand points worldwide. **No individual is monitored** for the system to work.
*See: §3.2, §4.3, §9 / Part 5*

### B5. Doesn't rewarding reductions punish people who already lived frugally?

That is why the dividend rewards **low levels of use first** and reductions only second. Someone who has always used little is rewarded for that level, not left behind because they have nothing more to cut.
*See: §4.3 / Part 5*

### B6. Canada had rebates and still repealed its carbon charge. Why would this be different?

In Canada, most households were estimated to receive more than they paid, yet few people felt it. Planetary OS treats visibility as part of the design:

- a clearly named payment, deposited directly on a fixed date
- a public simulator showing each household's net position
- small pilots that let people experience the dividend before the political fight

It also adds a launch-phase top-up from the PTE, paid for by past beneficiaries, so the gain is larger and easier to see.
*See: §4.3 / Parts 5, 7*

### B7. Why does the money go to citizens rather than governments, which could invest it?

There are three reasons:

- Revenue held by governments becomes a magnet for vested interests.
- When the money disappears into a budget, people feel it was simply taken from them.
- No central authority knows exactly where to invest.

Direct return lets millions of households adapt in their own way. Investment is not abandoned: the PTE invests and provides guarantees, funded separately by BEI.
*See: §3.2, §4.3 / Part 5*

## C. BEI and historical liability

### C1. Why should today's wealthy pay for emissions from decades ago?

BEI does not punish individuals for past acts. It recognizes that large concentrations of wealth and corporate profits were **built on** the fossil economy, and that the cost of that economy was left to the atmosphere and to future generations. The levy is thin (about 0.3–0.5% on the wealth of the top 1% and about 0.3% on corporate profits, as illustrations) and broad, so no single group carries a heavy load. It falls on large wealth and profits, **not on ordinary taxpayers**.
*See: §4.1 / Parts 2, 6*

### C2. Won't the wealthy just move their money elsewhere?

Some will try. As with the global minimum corporate tax, club members agree on a **minimum BEI rate**, so moving between member countries gains nothing. The larger the club, the fewer places remain to hide. Capital flight is listed in the risk register (§9) as a real risk, not one we consider solved.
*See: §4.1, §9 / Part 6*

### C3. Is around USD 1 trillion a year realistic?

That figure assumes global adoption. At the club stage, members' share would be several hundred billion dollars a year. Both are orders of magnitude for planning, not forecasts. BEI also starts thin and broad and adds liability attribution and legal recovery only as data and institutions mature.
*See: §4.1 / Part 6*

### C4. Why spend BEI on guarantees rather than grants to developing countries?

One of the largest obstacles to clean energy in developing countries is the high cost of capital. A guarantee that absorbs the first losses or currency risk can bring in several times its value in private investment. Grants still have a role, especially for climate-vulnerable countries, and the PTE funds both.
*See: §4.1 / Part 6*

### C5. Won't guarantees encourage reckless projects (moral hazard)?

The PTE limits that risk in six ways:

- It covers only a first-loss tranche; investors and developers bear the rest.
- It covers only risks developers cannot control, such as currency shocks, abrupt policy change, and offtaker default. Construction or management failure is not covered.
- Guarantee fees depend on risk and track record.
- It underwrites portfolios with rule-based eligibility checks.
- If a host government breaches its commitments, the PTE seeks recovery from it and reduces its club benefits.
- It sets an annual loss budget and publishes all losses.

*See: §4.1 / Part 6*

## D. The economy and industry

### D1. Won't industry simply move to countries without a cap (carbon leakage)?

Imports from outside the club face the same carbon cost at the border, so relocating to avoid the cap gains nothing in the club market. A non-member faces a simple choice: collect that revenue at home and return it to its own citizens, or pay it at someone else's border. Exports are harder. The main answer is to grow the club quickly so that it becomes the primary market. Export rebates require careful review under trade rules.
*See: §5.3, §5.4 / Part 6*

### D2. Isn't "sufficiency" just a polite word for recession and unemployment?

A recession is an uncontrolled contraction in which losses fall on whoever is weakest. Planetary OS designs a controlled transition:

- Only the outer boundary is strict. Life and business within it remain free.
- Repair, sharing, durable products, and local circular economies are rewarded.
- Workers and regions in fossil industries receive Just Transition support.

Industry splits into losers (legacy fossil-dependent firms) and winners (new low-impact sectors). The winners, together with citizens, become the constituency that defends the system.
*See: §3.2, §4.3, §5.4 / Parts 3, 5*

### D3. What happens if the carbon price spikes?

The cap does not loosen. Instead, the dividend and transition support rise automatically, which they do in any case because revenue rises with the price. Investment in alternatives is brought forward, and scarce supply is prioritized for essential uses. A price spike is the hardest test of public support, and this is the point where the dividend matters most. A price that is low, by contrast, means reductions are coming easily, and the cap's decline accelerates.
*See: §4.2 / Part 3*

### D4. Why not allow offsets? They lower costs.

Offsets let emissions above the cap be cancelled with reductions bought elsewhere. Those reductions are often hard to verify and may not be additional. Under a binding carbon budget, the cap has to mean what it says. Removing carbon from the atmosphere is funded separately, through BEI and the PTE, not traded against the cap.
*See: §4.2 / Part 3*

### D5. Fixing the rules at launch will lock in mistakes.

The rules are fixed against **loosening**, not against learning. The system keeps correcting course based on observed data: a low price accelerates the cap, and planetary-indicator alerts tighten it. Rules can be amended through the double key. What is made deliberately hard is weakening the cap or changing the terms of membership after the fact. That predictability is what makes "join now" rational and unlocks long-term investment.
*See: §4.4, §5.6 / Parts 3, 6*

## E. International scaling

### E1. Why would large emitters and fossil-fuel exporters ever join?

The design assumes that fossil-fuel exporters **will not join at first**. The natural first movers are fossil-fuel importers, climate-vulnerable nations, insurers and long-term investors, and ambitious cities and states. As the club grows, staying out becomes more costly: border carbon costs, finance and insurance that use the Standard as a yardstick, and historical-liability data that can support legal claims. How large a market the founding club can bring in is decisive. We do not claim to know how fast this will happen.
*See: §5.3, §5.8 / Part 6*

### E2. Isn't a border carbon adjustment protectionism? Does it break trade rules?

It applies the same carbon cost to domestic and imported goods, which is the opposite of discrimination in intent. Compatibility with trade law is a real question, though, and especially so for export treatment. A legal working group will review it early, learning from the EU's experience with its own carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM).
*See: §5.4, §9 / Part 6*

### E3. Isn't this rich countries imposing conditions on poor ones?

The design runs the other way:

- The cap declines more gradually for developing countries.
- Historical liability, paid mainly by wealth concentrated in high-income economies, funds the PTE.
- The PTE's guarantees aim directly at the high cost of capital that holds back clean investment in the Global South.

Climate-vulnerable countries are among the natural first movers, and their voice in the PPA and the Club is part of the design.
*See: §4.1, §4.2, §5.2 / Part 6*

### E4. Why would high-income countries agree, if they are net contributors?

In pure cash terms they are. Their gains are indirect but substantial:

- Their firms become the main investors, builders, and insurers.
- A larger club means larger markets and less carbon leakage.
- Resource and supply-chain security improves.
- Climate damage that would spill back home is reduced.
- They lead in setting the standards.

And BEI falls on large wealth and corporate profits, not on their ordinary taxpayers.
*See: §5.2 / Part 6*

### E5. Couldn't a country keep the club small to keep targets low?

No. Each step up in the reduction rate is triggered by club size **or by a deadline, whichever comes first**. At each step the required rate is recalculated from the remaining budget, so slow expansion means a steeper path later, not an easier one.
*See: §5.5 / Part 6*

### E6. Doesn't requiring countries to pay a carbon dividend interfere in their domestic affairs?

No. Under international law, intervention means coercing a state over matters it is entitled to decide for itself. Joining the club is voluntary and decided by each country through its own legislature, so accepting its rules is an exercise of sovereignty, as with the global minimum corporate tax or trade agreements. The club also agrees only on outcomes: most of the revenue goes back to households, at least 70% of households come out ahead, and the amounts and methods are published. Whether the money is returned as a direct dividend, a tax credit, or a top-up to existing benefits is each country's choice. Two legal points still need care. Some constitutions and budget rules limit the earmarking of revenue, which the flexible delivery is meant to accommodate. And the border adjustment must respect trade law (see E2). The legal working group will review both.
*See: §5.7 / Part 6*

### E7. Why make returning the revenue a common rule? Shouldn't members share only the cap, and decide for themselves how to use the money?

Because in this design, returning the revenue is not a spending preference. It is what keeps the cap in place. Carbon prices have repeatedly been reversed, in Australia, France, and Canada, because households felt the higher prices but could not see what they got back. A shared cap without a shared return would invite the same failure. When most households come out ahead, public opinion defends the cap instead of pushing it down.

This matters to every member, not just the country concerned. Club members cut emissions on the understanding that others will too. If one country uses the revenue elsewhere, faces a backlash, and loosens its cap, the others lose their reason to keep their commitments. A common rule lets members assure one another that each cap will hold, much as the global minimum corporate tax prevents a race to the bottom. It also ensures that, in every member country, lower-income households are not left carrying the burden.

The rule is kept to outcomes: most of the revenue goes back to households, at least 70% come out ahead, and the methods are published. How to return it is each country's choice (see E6). The choice of design itself is made once, when the Standard is drawn up: following the Charter, the World Citizens' Assembly compares this design with alternatives, such as a carbon-price-centered design that leaves the use of revenue to each country. Once a club adopts the Standard, every member follows the same common rules; members cannot pick different designs within the same club. A country that wants full freedom over the revenue can argue for a different design in that comparison, or choose not to join. Returning the revenue is the core of this design, not a claim that no other design deserves consideration.

Returning the revenue is necessary but not sufficient. In Canada, most households received rebates, yet in a January 2024 poll only about half of those eligible believed they had received one. That is why this design also makes the return visible and adds the majority-benefit rule.
*See: §5.7 / Part 5 / Part 6*

## F. Governance and democracy

### F1. "Automatic" sounds like technocracy. Who is accountable?

Politics decides the goals and the rules: the pace of the cap, differentiation between countries, the price corridor, alert thresholds, dividend ratios, oversight of the measuring bodies, and compensation. The system only executes those pre-approved rules day to day. This is the same division of labor as an independent central bank operating within a mandate set by elected bodies, with stronger citizen participation added.
*See: §3.2, §4.4 / Parts 2, 4*

### F2. Why should unelected people chosen by lot have binding power?

Citizens chosen by lot form a cross-section of society that elections often fail to produce, and they are not dependent on donors or the next election. Their role is specific: approving rule changes as one half of the double key, referring questions to referendum, and proposing legislation. They do not run the system. The legitimacy of this role, and how it relates to national parliaments, is an open question we want to test in Stage 2.
*See: §4.4 / Part 4*

### F3. Who guards the guardians? Couldn't the PPA be captured?

There are four safeguards:

- **Three layers.** Science, citizens, and open-source engineering check each other.
- **Separation.** Rule-setting (the Club) and money (the PTE) are kept apart.
- **Double key.** Rule changes need both scientific justification and approval by citizens' representatives.
- **Transparency.** Data is recorded and cross-verified across universities, research institutes, and civic nodes worldwide, and decision records and accounts are published.

*See: §4.4, §9 / Parts 2, 4*

### F4. What if the data is wrong and triggers a false alarm?

The alert system combines three kinds of indicators: cumulative levels, rates of change, and system health. Data is cross-verified across independent nodes. The most drastic step, a temporary circuit breaker on specific activities, applies only at the red level.
*See: §4.2, §9 / Part 3*

## G. About this initiative

### G1. This is the work of one person. Why should anyone trust it?

It should not be trusted as it stands. It is published as a **working draft**, meant to be rewritten through expert and civic review. Stage 1 is precisely that: forming working groups in economics, science, and law, drafting the Standard together, and testing it in a citizens' assembly. Outputs are published as a shared standard, not under any single name.
*See: §7, §11 / Parts 7, 8*

### G2. Isn't this utopian?

Every element already exists somewhere: cap-and-trade, citizen dividends, border carbon adjustments, guarantees, citizens' assemblies, and future-generations institutions. What is new is putting them together so that each covers the others' weaknesses. The first steps are modest and concrete: a written Standard, a public simulator, and small pilots. And when a climate shock pushes countries to act, the speed of the response will depend on whether a working alternative is already designed and tested.
*See: §6.4, §7, §13 / Parts 7, 8*

### G3. Why not simply support existing climate efforts?

This does not replace them. Most climate resources have gone into raising the alarm and petitioning governments for better laws, and that work matters. What has been missing is an integrated blueprint for the system to move to. Planetary OS offers one, and it changes the question put to politics from "please act" to "**will you connect to this?**"
*See: §2.3, §6.3 / Part 7*

### G4. How can I check whether my household would come out ahead?

Use the **Citizen Dividend Simulator** [→](../tools/simulator.md). Enter your household size and how much energy you use compared with the average. You can also change the assumptions, such as the carbon price, the share returned to citizens, the PTE top-up, and the majority-benefit rule, and see how the result changes.
*See: §4.3 / Part 5*

## H. Open questions we have not solved

Some objections point to problems that remain open. We list them here rather than hide them:

- **Exports.** Protecting exporters without breaking trade rules has no settled answer yet.
- **Speed of club growth.** Whether the club can grow fast enough to stay within the 2°C budget is uncertain.
- **Legal basis of BEI.** Its form under national and international law, and how far liability attribution can go, needs legal work.
- **Measurement for the sufficiency-linked dividend.** Exactly which data, and what adjustments, need testing in pilots.
- **Legitimacy of citizens' representatives.** How their binding role relates to existing parliaments is unsettled.
- **The ratios themselves.** Every number in this proposal is an illustration. The real values should come from scientific review and democratic deliberation.

These are the questions Stage 1 is designed to work on. If you have expertise in any of them, we would like to hear from you.
