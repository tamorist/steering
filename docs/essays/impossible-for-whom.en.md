# "Realistically Impossible" — For Whom?
### Why the rules that serve citizens have to be drafted by citizens


**Essay** — First edition (working draft)


**A note from the author.** I am not a specialist in economics or climate policy. This essay was developed with generative AI as a sparring partner for structuring the ideas and checking them against existing research. The judgments, and the responsibility for them, are mine. Critical comments are very welcome.

## Everyone has been trying

Ten years have passed since the Paris Agreement was adopted.

In that time, a great many people have tried hard. Negotiators have worked through the night to close agreements. Researchers have sharpened their observations and forecasts. Young people have taken to the streets, companies have set decarbonization targets, and the price of renewable energy has fallen sharply.

Even so, global emissions remain stuck near their peak. In 2024, for the first time on record, the average temperature for a year was more than 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels (about 1.55°C, according to the WMO).

Was the effort not enough? This essay does not think so.

In 2018, the yellow vest movement spread across France in protest at a rise in fuel taxes. One of its placards read:

> "The elites talk about the end of the world. We talk about the end of the month."

This line can be read not as an accusation, but as a clue to what was missing. The people worried about the end of the world and the people worried about the end of the month are both right. What was missing was a **design** that handles both concerns within the same system.

And something has kept that design from being written: a phrase we have all heard many times. *"It's realistically impossible."*

---

## Three designs that were missing

### 1. Rules that make staying out pay

Cutting emissions has a well-known difficulty. A country that cuts alone bears the cost, while the benefit is spread thinly across the whole world. If others cut, each country does better by carrying on. If everyone thinks this way, everyone ends up worse off: the familiar "prisoner's dilemma."

The Paris Agreement lets each country set its own targets and reviews progress every five years. There is no penalty for missing a target. This was a deliberate choice, made after the Kyoto Protocol's binding targets failed to keep the major emitters on board. As a compromise to bring everyone to the same table, it had good reasons.

What was needed next, though, was **a system in which taking part and cutting emissions is the better deal**. That has not yet been built. The agreement was a way in, not a way out.

### 2. No decision on who bears the cost

Many economists have long said that a carbon tax, which puts a price on emissions, is the most efficient tool. In theory, they are right.

But France's fuel-tax increase hit hardest the households in rural areas who cannot live without a car. It set off large protests and was withdrawn (see Part 1 of the series [→](../series/part-1.md)). The theory was not wrong. What had not been designed carefully enough was **who would pay**.

For a family whose plans for the month are upset by a small rise in fuel prices, this month's bill naturally matters more than temperatures decades from now. That is not a lack of awareness. It is the reality of their lives. When the pain of a transition is concentrated on the people least able to bear it, even the most correct policy loses support.

The lesson often drawn from this episode is: "So large changes are impossible." The real lesson is different. **The policy failed because it was designed to pass the cost on to ordinary households.** This difference matters a great deal later on.

### 3. The people who decide and the people affected are far apart

The rules of climate policy are set in international conferences, government committees, and research institutes. Most of the people who work there are serious, capable, and well-meaning.

Yet the people most affected by those policies rarely have a seat there: the worker at a coal mine that is closing, the owner of a factory shutting down because of energy prices, the rural household shaken by the cost of fuel.

This is not a problem of individuals. It is a problem of structure: **whoever sits in that seat, some things are hard to see from it.** The figures and models visible from a meeting room and the weight of the bills visible from a kitchen table are two sides of the same reality. If the second is not built into the design, the system stalls the moment it meets reality, however refined the first may be.

## Questioning what has been called "impossible"

### The principle has already been shown

So has no one worked out a system in which staying out costs more than joining? They have. The principle has already been shown, both in theory and in practice.

**In theory.** In 2015, William Nordhaus, who later received the 2018 Nobel Prize in economics, published the idea of a "climate club." Member countries adopt a common carbon price and put a tariff on imports from countries that stay out. His analysis showed that agreements without penalties produce only minimal cuts, but that attaching even a small cost to staying out can sustain a large and stable coalition.

Nordhaus's version, however, put **the same tariff on all imports** from a non-member. That would penalize the cleanest producers in that country along with the rest, and would place the same burden on poor countries that cannot yet afford a carbon price. On fairness, it has serious problems.

**In current policy.** The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), which began full operation in 2026, charges imports of steel, aluminium, cement, and some other goods according to the emissions released in making them. Because the standard is the emissions of the product, not the country it comes from, a producer in any country pays less if it produces cleanly. And if a carbon price has already been paid in the exporting country, that amount is deducted. This creates an incentive: "Rather than pay the EU, we would do better to introduce our own carbon price." In fact, China has announced plans to extend its national emissions trading system to steel, cement, and aluminium, and CBAM is cited as one reason.

CBAM's problem is not fairness but scope. It covers only a few materials, and products made from them were initially excluded. The EU is discussing an extension, but it has a long way to go.

**A successful precedent.** The Montreal Protocol (1987), which protected the ozone layer, is regarded as the most successful international environmental treaty. It restricted trade with non-parties in controlled substances and in products containing them. A country that stayed out but could show it was complying was exempted. And developing countries received help with the costs of transition through a Multilateral Fund.

What these show is that **a system in which staying out does not pay can be built, if differences are drawn on a fair standard and combined with support for the transition**: judging by conduct rather than membership, covering enough of the economy, and supporting developing countries through the change. What remains is to apply that principle to climate with enough breadth and strength. Proposal C, Planetary OS, is one attempt to do so [→](../proposals/c-summary.md).

### The familiar answer: "It's realistically impossible"

Yet whenever someone proposes to widen and strengthen such a system, the same objection comes back. The usual example is the United States and China. Together they account for over 40 percent of world GDP. If they push back and retaliate, prices will rise and the economies that adopted the system will suffer first. So it is impossible, the argument goes.

It is not only border measures. Whenever a large change is discussed, the same words return:

"I understand the ideal. But realistically, it's impossible."
"Do that, and you will wreck the economy."
"In the end, it's ordinary people who will suffer."

Politicians say it, economic experts say it, commentators on television say it. And at some point, we citizens began to say it ourselves.

This is the point this essay most wants to make. What has held back progress is neither a lack of technology nor a lack of public awareness. It is **this "realistically impossible" logic itself**, and that logic does not represent the interests of citizens. Let us check this step by step.

### Taking the premise apart

Large changes always have costs. Changing the energy system, rebuilding industries, rethinking supply chains that have depended on other countries: prices rise and some jobs are lost. Up to this point, it is a matter of fact.

The problem is the next step. The conclusion "impossible" follows only from one premise: **that ordinary households will bear those costs as they are.**

The claim that US and Chinese retaliation will raise prices has the same structure. If households absorb rising prices as they are, it is indeed unbearable. But that is not a law of nature. It is a design choice about where the costs go.

As France showed, if you start from the premise that costs fall on households, every large change will always be "impossible." Turn that around, and once the premise is removed, much of the "impossible" stops being impossible.

### "The economy" is not one thing

When people say "it will wreck the economy," whose economy do they mean?

In 2022, Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent energy prices soaring. Households everywhere struggled with energy bills, and governments stepped in with subsidies. In the same year, the largest Western oil companies reported record or near-record profits: about $56 billion for ExxonMobil and about $40 billion for Shell. Britain and others placed special taxes on these windfall profits.

Also in 2022, explicit subsidies for fossil fuels worldwide reached $1.3 trillion (IMF estimate, about 2.6 times the 2020 level). And roughly half of the emissions linked to the world's consumption are estimated to come from the richest 10 percent of people (Oxfam, based on 2019 data).

What this shows is that **"the economy" is not a single block.** In every crisis, some parts of it absorb the pain, and other parts collect the surplus. And when people say "the economy will break," they often mean that the share going to the second group will shrink.

### What do citizens gain from keeping things as they are?

So when "it's impossible, so let's keep things as they are" is chosen, what do citizens get?

- Energy price spikes happen anyway. 2022 showed that.
- Disasters, heatwaves, and crop losses from climate change will keep growing down that road.
- And each time, the cost will again be passed on to households.

Keeping things as they are is not a "painless option" for citizens. It is **an option that postpones the pain and keeps passing it to the same people.**

"If we change too fast, ordinary people will suffer" sounds, at first, like an effort to protect citizens. But the premise behind it is a design in which "ordinary people pay," and no intention to change that design. As a result, **citizens' very real worries end up serving as the reason not to change anything.**

Citizens have no reason to keep playing that role.

### Who says "impossible"? A conflict of interest, not bad faith

To avoid any misunderstanding: this essay does not claim that the people who say "impossible" are lying in bad faith.

When the premise changes, the people whose burden grows are those who gain the most from today's system, and those who have built their position and influence within it. And the people who have examined the policy options and judged what is "realistic" have often been the same people.

In every field, a basic rule of fair procedure is that people step aside from decisions in which they have a large stake. A judge does not hear a relative's case; a referee does not officiate their own team's match. That is not because the person would cheat. It is because **for anyone, it is hard to judge as "possible" an option under which they stand to lose a great deal.**

Much of what is called "realistically impossible" marks not the limits of the economy, but a line drawn from within this conflict of interest. In other words, it reflects **where the speaker stands**. It is not a judgment made on behalf of citizens' interests.

We also often hear: "If you ask the wealthy and companies to pay, investment will flee, and ordinary people will suffer in the end." This argument, too, is usually made by those who would be asked to pay. And what happened in 2022 was that the cost of the crisis was already being passed to ordinary people.

### What becomes possible when the premise changes

Change the premise to "**costs are borne by those with the capacity to bear them**," and options that were dismissed as "impossible" come back to the table. For example:

- Those who have collected the surplus, and those responsible for most past emissions, carry more of the cost of the transition, and the revenue goes back directly to households as a dividend
- Workers and regions that depend on fossil fuels receive support for the change before it reaches them
- Energy made closer to home, from renewable sources, makes households less exposed to fuel-price shocks and to trade retaliation

On that footing, growing a fair border measure such as CBAM to sufficient breadth and strength becomes a real option too. These are the directions Planetary OS takes, through a one-way cap on fossil fuels and a citizen dividend [→](../proposals/c-summary.md).

None of this is easy. But none of it is technically impossible. What made it impossible was the premise about who pays.

## What the drafting needs

From this diagnosis, we can see what a new framework needs, especially in **how it is written and by whom**. (The essay *Why Can't Humanity Stop the Slide?* [→](missing-institution.md) sets out five conditions that any institution deciding for the planet must meet. The conditions here are about something else: the drafting of the rules themselves.)

1. **Write in the language of daily life.** Not abstract targets, but electricity bills, rent, food, and jobs: the things people deal with every day.
2. **Decide first where the costs go.** Make it the starting point of the design that costs do not concentrate on the most vulnerable: who pays how much, and who gets what back.
3. **Make taking part pay, on a fair standard.** Do not rely only on goodwill or reputation; build in a structure where joining and cutting emissions pay. Draw any difference on emissions and conduct, not on which country a producer belongs to, and pair it with support for developing countries' transition.
4. **Bring the people affected into the drafting.** Those most affected by a policy take part from the stage at which the rules are made.
5. **Bring in experts as partners who supply knowledge.** The knowledge of researchers and practitioners is essential. But the lead in deciding priorities rests with the people affected.
6. **Show the dangers honestly, without softening them.** Speaking of hope matters, but it is not the same as making reality look smaller. Real agreement comes only when the size of the change needed is shared honestly.

## Citizens' interests have to be drafted by citizens

The existing international negotiations have carried out the extremely difficult task of turning the agreement of more than 190 countries into text. That role will remain important.

But as we have seen, the people who have judged what is "possible" are themselves deeply invested in today's system. It is hard to expect a framework that greatly increases their own burden to come from that setting. Not because anyone is lazy, but because of how the structure works.

If so, **a framework that serves citizens' interests has to be drafted by citizens themselves.** The knowledge of experts and practitioners is indispensable, and they join as a force supporting the drafting. But the pen that decides what counts as "possible," and how costs are shared, should be held by those who have been made to bear the costs so far.

This does not replace states. The options drafted by citizens are handed to governments, parliaments, and international negotiations, which keep the power to adopt and implement them. What changes is who writes the first draft. Proposal A, Youth GCC [→](../proposals/a-youth-gcc.md), describes one way to do this: a citizens' assembly chosen by lot, with experts as advisers and the final judgment resting with the citizens.

This is not a fantasy. In Serbia, the collapse of a station canopy in November 2024, which killed 16 people, set off protests led by university students. In the parliamentary election of October 2026, the "Student List" born of that movement defeated the long-ruling party (confirmed on 5 November: 140 of 250 seats). Students themselves were not among the candidates. The list was made up of professors, doctors, farmers, athletes, artists, and others rooted in their own fields.

It is not a climate case. But it shows that when the people affected become the actors themselves, things long called "impossible" can move.

## Will you help write it?

Based on the conditions above, we are drafting a package of proposals for a new framework: *Steering Within Limits* [→](../package.md).

We do not want to hand over something finished. We want to write it together.

- **If you feel the weight of prices and energy bills in your daily life:** your bills and the reality of your work are the starting point of the design.
- **If you work on the front line** of energy, manufacturing, farming, or logistics: please tell us what is happening there.
- **If you are a researcher or expert:** please use your knowledge to find the holes in these proposals.
- **If you are young:** you will live with the effects of this framework the longest.

How to take part: [→](../join.md)

What was missing was not will, but design. If so, the design can be rewritten.

The next time you hear "it's realistically impossible," try asking back, just once:

**Impossible for whom?**
