# Why Doesn't Technology Make Life Easier?
### Where efficiency gains disappear, and how we could get them back

**Background essay 1** — First edition (working draft)


---

Technology keeps getting better. Computers are thousands of times faster than a generation ago, and AI can now draft, translate, and analyze in seconds. Factories, logistics, and offices are more efficient than ever.

So why do so many people feel busier and more pressed than before? Why are working hours not much shorter, and why does daily life feel harder rather than easier?

This essay argues that the answer is not a lack of effort, talent, or technology. It is the way our economic rules turn efficiency gains into a faster race instead of a better life. And because those are rules, they can be changed.

---

## 1. Gains that vanish into the race

Imagine a company that introduces AI into customer support and cuts the time per inquiry to a tenth.

- **At first**, costs fall and staff have some breathing room.
- **Within months**, competitors adopt the same tool.
- **Soon**, instant, 24-hour, personalized answers become the new normal. Customers expect them, and every firm has to provide them.
- **In the end**, margins return to where they were. Staff now handle ten times as many interactions, plus the work of tuning the system. The time and money saved have gone into a higher standard of service that no one can opt out of.

This is the everyday version of a pattern economists have long noticed: efficiency gains often lead to more activity rather than less. When every firm gets faster at the same time, no one is ahead, and everyone has to keep running.

---

## 2. The race for attention

Digital technology has brought the cost of producing text, images, and video close to zero. But one thing has not grown at all: **human attention**. There are still twenty-four hours in a day, and each of us can take in only so much.

When supply becomes almost unlimited and attention stays fixed, the cost of getting noticed rises. Content that takes a minute to create may need ten times as much spent on advertising, search optimization, and promotion to reach anyone. Much of the effort saved in production is spent again in the competition for eyes.

---

## 3. Where the surplus goes

If gains vanish at the level of firms, why do statistics still show growth? Because the surplus does not disappear. It flows elsewhere, mostly away from daily life.

**Into assets.** Profits increasingly go to share buybacks and dividends, and surplus capital flows into shares, property, and land. Asset prices rise, and so do rents and house prices. Those who own assets gain; those who need a home pay more.

**Into friction that is counted as growth.** Advertising, intermediary fees, and the cost of competing for attention all count as economic activity. GDP measures how much money changes hands, not whether lives are better. More friction can look like more growth.

**Into prices that never seem to fall.** Technology makes many goods cheaper, such as electronics and clothing. Meanwhile, monetary policy in most countries aims at steady, modest inflation, and the costs that matter most, such as housing, education, health care, and care for children and the elderly, keep rising.

**Into fixed costs of living.** Things that were once provided by communities or the public sector, from housing support to local services, have increasingly become monthly bills. Even when individual products get cheaper, the total we must pay just to live keeps growing.

The result is a strange combination: an economy that grows on paper while many households feel squeezed.

---

## 4. No time to think

This situation also makes it harder to change.

When rent, education, and health care take most of a household's income, many people are one missed paycheck away from crisis. Research on scarcity suggests that constant financial pressure uses up mental bandwidth, leaving less room to plan ahead, question how things work, or organize with others. **Pressure on time and money is also pressure on citizenship.**

At the same time, economic rules tend to feel like laws of nature. "That's just how markets work" is easy to accept, and frustration is more easily directed at visible targets, such as a particular politician, a group of people, or "the system" in the abstract, than at the specific rules that could be changed: working time, accounting standards, and how revenue is shared.

---

## 5. Not a shortage, but a runaway

Put these pieces together, and a clear picture emerges. Humanity produces more food, goods, and knowledge than ever. Materially, we could all have more time and security than we do.

The problem is not scarcity. It is a set of rules under which **everyone acting sensibly produces a collective failure**, like a theater audience in which each person stands on tiptoe to see better, until everyone is tired and no one sees any better. (The essay *Why Now?* explores this at the scale of the planet.)

---

## 6. Two shifts in thinking

Two changes in how we think about the economy open the way to a solution.

**First: wealth built on unpaid costs carries responsibility.** Much of today's accumulated wealth was built while the cost of using up the atmosphere and ecosystems was treated as zero. That does not make anyone a villain. But it does mean that part of that wealth rests on costs pushed onto others and onto the future. Asking it to contribute to a safe transition is not confiscation. It is settling an account that was never closed. (Background essay 2 develops this idea.)

**Second: the economy is a set of rules we can redesign.** Money, accounting, taxes, and working hours are human agreements. We can set them to serve the goals we choose, such as security, free time, and a livable planet, instead of treating ever-faster growth as a law of nature.

---

## 7. Getting the gains back

What would it look like to return efficiency gains to people instead of to the race?

**As money.** Planetary OS, the first agenda of the Youth GCC, returns most of the revenue from a cap on fossil fuels directly to citizens. Because energy use rises steeply with income, most households come out ahead, and those who use less gain the most.

**As time.** Gains from AI and automation could be shared as shorter standard working hours, introduced step by step and by social agreement, rather than as ever more output. Four-day-week trials in several countries have reported that many organizations maintained their results while staff wellbeing improved. Designing this fairly across sectors and for small businesses needs care, and it should be tested before it spreads.

**As security.** A transition endowment funded by a thin levy on the largest fortunes can invest in alternatives and cushion the shocks of change, so that slowing the race does not mean falling into crisis.

None of this requires anyone to work harder or live with less joy. It requires changing where the gains go.

---

## Closing: stop running faster, and change the rules of the race

We have been told that if we work harder and innovate faster, life will get better. For many, the opposite has happened. That is not a personal failure. It is the predictable result of rules that turn every gain into a faster race.

The first step is to see the rules clearly. The second is to rewrite them, starting with the parameters that matter most: who pays for the use of the planet, where the revenue goes, and how much of our lives we spend at work.
