# The Next Layers of Planetary OS
### Accounting, a financial safety net, and time

**Background essay 2** — First edition (working draft)


---

Planetary OS addresses the most urgent layer of the problem: keeping the economy within the carbon budget while making reduction pay. But beneath it lies a deeper rule: **the economy has to keep expanding**, or debts go unpaid, jobs disappear, and finance becomes unstable.

As long as that rule stands, every serious attempt to use less will feel like a threat. This essay sets out three further layers that could, over time, loosen it:

1. **Accounting** that recognizes ecological liabilities
2. **A financial safety net** so that slowing growth does not trigger a crisis
3. **Returning productivity as time**, through gradually shorter working hours

These are directions for future deliberation, not part of the Youth GCC's first agenda. Like everything in Planetary OS, the details are illustrative and meant to be tested and revised.

---

## 1. Why slowing down is frightening

In today's economy, slowing down is dangerous for reasons that have little to do with anyone's preferences:

- **Debt** is repaid out of future income. If incomes stop growing, defaults rise.
- **Asset prices** assume future growth. If expectations fall, so do pension funds and savings.
- **Jobs** depend on rising demand. Productivity gains without growth mean fewer jobs, unless working time falls too.

This is why calls for "degrowth" or "sufficiency" frighten many people, and why politicians fear them. A transition that ignores these mechanisms will be reversed. A transition that redesigns them can last.

---

## 2. Layer 1: accounting for ecological liability

### The problem

When a firm calculates its profit, it pays for energy, materials, and labor. For two centuries, one cost has been set at zero: the use of the atmosphere and ecosystems to absorb emissions and waste. Had that cost been paid, much of today's accumulated capital would be smaller, or would not exist.

### Three lines of argument

Legal scholars, economists, and ethicists have advanced several arguments for treating part of this wealth as carrying an unpaid obligation. They remain debated, and none is settled law, but they help explain why the question matters:

- **Unjust enrichment.** Gains obtained by using something of value without paying for it, in this case the atmosphere's limited capacity, belonging to everyone including future generations, can carry an obligation to return part of the benefit.
- **A continuing harm.** CO₂ emitted decades ago is still in the atmosphere, still causing damage today. The responsibility is therefore not only historical; the harm is ongoing.
- **Privatized gains, socialized costs.** When profits are private but the resulting damage is borne by society and the future, the principle of personal responsibility, central to market economies, is not being applied consistently.

### What accounting could do

One proposal is for firms and large holders of capital to **disclose their accumulated ecological liabilities** alongside their other liabilities. In simplified form:

> accumulated liability ≈ Σ (emissions in each year × social cost of carbon in that year) − environmental taxes already paid

Even as disclosure alone, without any payment, this would show investors and the public how much of a firm's value rests on unpaid costs. Climate-related disclosure is already becoming standard practice: international sustainability disclosure standards published in 2023 ask companies to report climate risks and emissions. Accounting for cumulative liability would be a further step in the same direction.

### How Planetary OS starts

Planetary OS does not begin with firm-by-firm liability or litigation, which could mean years of conflict. It begins with **BEI: a thin, broad levy** on the largest fortunes and corporate profits, held in the Planetary Transition Endowment (PTE). This is not punishment for past actions, and it is not confiscation. It recognizes that part of today's wealth rests on costs pushed onto others, and asks it to help pay for a safe transition. More precise attribution can follow as data and institutions mature.

## 3. Layer 2: a financial safety net

### The problem

As fossil fuels are phased out and growth slows in high-impact sectors, some loans will go bad and some assets will lose value: coal plants, pipelines, and businesses built on them. If those losses hit banks and pension funds all at once, a financial crisis could follow, and the transition would be blamed.

### The idea

A **buffer fund** could absorb part of these shocks in an orderly way. The PTE, funded by BEI, is a natural candidate. It could, for example:

- buy out and retire fossil-fuel assets early, on condition that they close on schedule. Development banks have begun testing this approach for coal plants in Asia.
- help restructure debts tied to stranded assets, so that losses are spread over time rather than triggering a chain of defaults
- support workers and regions through the Just Transition, so that slowing down does not mean losing a livelihood

### Guarding against moral hazard

A safety net must not reward reckless lending. The same principles as the PTE's guarantees apply:

- support only for losses caused by the transition itself, not by poor management
- losses shared by lenders and owners
- published rules and records
- an annual limit

The goal is not to protect anyone from all losses. It is to make sure that slowing growth does not become a crisis that hurts everyone, and that turns the public against the transition.

## 4. Layer 3: returning productivity as time

### The problem

When productivity rises and output does not, fewer hours of work are needed. Under today's rules, that shows up as layoffs for some and overwork for others. The alternative is to share the gains as **time**.

### The idea

Gains from AI and automation could be returned through **gradually shorter standard working hours**, introduced by social agreement, sector by sector, rather than being consumed by ever more output and overcompetition. Because every firm moves together, no firm is put at a disadvantage, in the same way that a common cap removes the "reducing alone means losing" problem.

### What we know, and what we need to test

Trials of a four-day week in several countries have reported that many participating organizations maintained their results while staff wellbeing improved. But questions remain:

- how to protect incomes, especially for low-paid workers
- how to adapt the idea to sectors such as care, health, and small businesses
- how to keep shorter hours from turning into more intense work

These are exactly the kinds of questions the Youth GCC's World Policy Laboratory is designed to test.

## 5. How the layers fit together

| Layer | What it does | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Planetary OS core** | A one-way cap on fossil fuels; most revenue returned to citizens; BEI and the PTE | First agenda of the Youth GCC |
| **Accounting** | Makes unpaid ecological costs visible in the accounts of firms and capital | Later; starts with disclosure |
| **Financial safety net** | Absorbs the shocks of phasing out fossil assets and slowing growth | Later; builds on the PTE |
| **Time** | Returns productivity gains as shorter working hours | Later; tested in pilots first |

Related concepts developed separately, such as a Designed Model Economy, a Global Shrinkage Evaluation System, and a Dual Value Economy, explore the same direction: treating the economy's parameters as democratic choices rather than laws of nature.

---

## Closing: a soft landing, not a crash

The fear of slowing down is real, because today's rules make it dangerous. The answer is not to keep running, and not to stop suddenly. It is to **redesign the rules so that a soft landing is possible**: recognize the costs that were never paid, build a safety net for the transition, and share the gains of technology as time.

Planetary OS is the first step. These layers show where the path could lead.
